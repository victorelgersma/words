---
title: "Resist the QR-codification of spaces of leisure"
date: 2026-10-04
draft: false
---

With enough time and boredom I hope to one day write a full treatise arguing for the criminalisation of QR codes in spaces of leisure, such as bars, cafés, pubs, cinemas and the like. But I will give a brief sketch:

On a hot summer day in our favourite lakeside brasserie, the only thing standing between my dad and a chicken caesar salad with two lemonades were 10 consecutive finger taps in a janky HTML form on a laggy 3G connection. After getting frustrated with his inability to bend the form to his will, he looked at me in quiet resignation and said: "Am I really getting that old?".

No, dad, you are not getting that old. The indignity you suffered on your day off from work is due to a choice that society has collectively made. That older people don't protest vocally against the "QR codification" of spaces of leisure is probably due to their fear of being found out that they lack "21st century skills".

But what are these "skills" exactly, and why do I possess them whereas my dad does not? There is nothing virtuous about my ability to bend janky HTML forms to my will. The skills that I possess were picked up from video games, dodging ads on porn and illegal streaming sites, and avoiding sneaky surcharges on cheap airlines' Kafkaesque websites. These "21st century skills", craved by the old and abused by the young, are simply a coping strategy for surviving our vile little corner of late capitalism.

They are nothing to be proud of, and not something we should encourage.

QR codes, while admired by software engineers for being fast and fault tolerant when they work, in practice just mean the replacement of a 10 second conversation with 10 taps in a confusing, badly designed HTML form. When used in places of leisure they represent a subtle perversion of the social contract. QR codes may be clever, but putting them in spaces of leisure is, to use a term familiar to software engineers, is an "anti pattern", and bad for accessibility.

Ditching the smartphone makes you a quiet but powerful ally against the QR-codification of spaces of leisure.
